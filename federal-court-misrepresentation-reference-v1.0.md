> This is a research reference, not legal advice, not a prediction tool, not a win-rate study and not a statement that every procedural fairness letter requires the same disclosure. The cases below must be read in context and against current controlling legislation, regulations and official program guidance.

## Purpose

This reference is designed to help practitioners distinguish recurring procedural-fairness and materiality questions in immigration misrepresentation cases: how clearly a concern must be disclosed, when undisclosed external material can matter, why the content of fairness remains contextual, and how the adequacy of a materiality analysis under A40(1)(a) depends on the reasons and record before the Court.

The table deliberately places materially different Federal Court reasoning side by side rather than presenting favourable cases in isolation. In particular, *Vasilev* and *Ekpe* illustrate different outcomes on PFL adequacy, while *Chater* and *Righetti* illustrate different outcomes on the sufficiency of materiality reasoning.

## Current statutory and policy anchors

- **A40(1)(a), IRPA:** a permanent resident or a foreign national is inadmissible for directly or indirectly misrepresenting or withholding material facts relating to a relevant matter that induces or could induce an error in the administration of IRPA.
- **A16(1), IRPA:** a person who makes an application must answer truthfully all questions put for the purpose of the examination and must produce a visa and all relevant evidence and documents that the officer reasonably requires.
- **Current IRCC procedural-fairness guidance:** applicants must be informed of decision-maker concerns and have a meaningful opportunity to respond. The guidance states that applicants must be advised of significant facts likely to affect the outcome and, where a decision-maker relies on extrinsic evidence, must be advised of that evidence and given an opportunity to respond. It also states that procedural fairness does not necessarily require production of the physical documents consulted; the essence of the concern must be communicated and the applicant given a reasonable opportunity to respond.
- **Work-permit context:** R200(5) sets the genuineness factors for an offer of employment. In the applicable LMIA assessment framework, R203(1)(a) requires an assessment of whether the offer is genuine under R200(5). These provisions form part of the background in the work-permit cases below; the procedural-fairness propositions remain case-specific.

The Canada.ca guidance is departmental policy/procedure. It is not presented as legislation and does not displace the governing statutory text or the reasons in the cited judgments.

| **Vasilev v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 716** | TRV / misrepresentation / procedural fairness | Whether misrepresentation automatically places fairness at the highest end and whether the concern was disclosed clearly enough | The duty of fairness in misrepresentation cases is heightened but remains contextual under *Baker*; not every misrepresentation case is automatically situated at the high end. Where the decision maker has identified a specific concern, a PFL stating only a general fraud concern may be insufficient if it does not disclose the specific concern with enough clarity to permit a meaningful response. | *Vasilev* does not hold that every investigative detail must be disclosed in every case. | **Ekpe v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 727** | Work permit / misrepresentation / procedural fairness | Whether a PFL identifying a bank statement as likely fraudulent disclosed enough of the case to meet | Grammond J held that the process was fair on the record before the Court. A PFL stating that a bank document was likely fraudulent was sufficient to disclose the gist of the concern, and the officer was not required to disclose the precise issue that prompted the inquiry to the bank. | Direct comparator to *Vasilev*, not later treatment of it. The two decisions were issued on consecutive days and *Ekpe* does not cite *Vasilev*. PFL adequacy remains fact- and context-specific. | paras 4-6 |
| **Agyemang v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 30** | Study permit / misrepresentation / disclosure of actual concerns and extrinsic evidence | Whether the applicant received the officer's actual concerns and the material giving rise to them | Battista J held that, in the misrepresentation determination before the Court, procedural fairness required disclosure of the officer's actual concerns reflected in the file notes and disclosure of extrinsic evidence giving rise to those concerns, subject to justified limits such as privilege, national security, or circumstances warranting a summary. | Read together with *Vasilev* 2026 FC 716. Do not state that every misrepresentation case always requires full disclosure or automatically sits at the high end of the fairness spectrum. *Vasilev* later adopted a materially different contextual *Baker* analysis. | paras 47, 60, 62-63, 81-85, 92-93 |
| **Odobo v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 49** | LMIA-based work permit / misrepresentation / adequacy of PFL | Gap between generic PFL wording and materially more detailed concerns in GCMS notes | Applying *Agyemang*, Battista J found procedural unfairness where the PFL conveyed only a generic, minimal concern even though the GCMS notes recorded materially more detailed misrepresentation concerns; on those facts, the applicant was not given meaningful disclosure of the case to meet. | Read together with *Vasilev*. Do not present *Odobo* as establishing an uncontested universal disclosure standard. The underlying positive-LMIA/job-offer genuineness issue also engaged R203(1)(a) and R200(5), but the proposition stated here is limited to procedural fairness. | paras 10-17 |
| **Ju v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 236** | Saskatchewan nominee PR/work permit / employer documents / misrepresentation | New determinative employer concerns arising from documents apparently unavailable to the applicant | Where employer documents obtained after an interview generated determinative new concerns about the employer's commercial and human-resource circumstances, and the applicant did not appear to have access to those documents, procedural fairness was breached when the officers relied on those concerns without communicating them and giving the applicant a meaningful opportunity to respond. | The Court did not decide the Applicant's reasonableness arguments; it expressly stated that its silence should not be perceived as endorsing the reasonableness of the decisions. | paras 10, 15-24, 30-34 |
| **Chater v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 588** | TRV / A40(1)(a) materiality | Whether the officer adequately explained materiality of omitted prior refusals | Materiality under A40(1)(a) cannot simply be presumed. On the record before the Court, a bare conclusion that an omission could have induced an error in the administration of IRPA was insufficient where the officer did not explain how the omitted prior visa refusals met the materiality requirement. | The Court did not decide the separately argued innocent-mistake issue. This row concerns justification/materiality, not a procedural-fairness breach. Read with *Righetti* 2026 FC 830, where concise materiality reasons were upheld because the officer actually connected the prior refusals to the applicant's immigration history and a full assessment of eligibility/admissibility. | paras 5-10 |
| **Righetti v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 830** | eTA / omitted prior work-permit refusals / fairness and materiality | Whether a further PFL or interview was required after the applicant responded, and whether the materiality reasons were sufficient | On the record before the Court, the PFL identified the statutory concern, the relevant application question and the factual basis involving three prior work-permit refusals. Because the applicant's response put his knowledge and the plausibility of his explanation squarely in issue without creating a new concern, no further PFL or interview was required merely because the officer rejected the explanation. The Court also upheld the officer's concise materiality analysis because the reasons explained why the prior refusals mattered to a full assessment of the applicant's immigration history, eligibility and admissibility. | Comparator to *Chater* on omitted-refusal materiality, not later treatment of it. *Righetti* does not hold that materiality may be presumed or that reasons are unnecessary; it upheld concise reasons that actually articulated why the omitted information mattered. | paras 35-38, 50-54 |

## What this table does not say

- It does not establish that every PFL must reproduce the officer's complete investigative file.
- It does not establish that every misrepresentation case is automatically at the highest end of the procedural-fairness spectrum.
- It does not establish that full disclosure is the universally controlling standard in every misrepresentation case; *Vasilev*, *Ekpe* and current IRCC guidance demonstrate why the required disclosure must be assessed in context.
- It does not establish that a second PFL or interview is required every time an officer rejects an applicant's response; *Righetti* illustrates that a response can itself put knowledge and plausibility in issue without creating a new concern.
- It does not establish that every A40(1)(a) decision requires a lengthy standalone materiality analysis. *Chater* and *Righetti* demonstrate why the question is whether the reasons adequately justify materiality on the record before the Court.
- It does not establish that non-disclosure always leads to judicial review relief.
- It does not replace A40(1)(a), A16(1), the applicable regulations, current program instructions or the complete reasons in the cited judgments.
- It does not convert one case-specific holding into a universal evidentiary checklist.
- It is not an exhaustive catalogue of every Federal Court misrepresentation decision released by the research cutoff date; cases are selected for the specific disclosure/fairness/materiality mechanisms shown in the table.

## Methodology

1. Cases were selected from the MAK Immigration Case-Law Research Platform to address coherent misrepresentation/disclosure/materiality mechanisms and to preserve materially different outcomes and reasoning, not to present only applicant-success authorities.
2. Each proposition was checked against the cited paragraph pinpoints. The original rows were re-opened against official Federal Court decisions where accessible and rechecked on 2026-09-15 against current CanLII full text and live court-file metadata. *Ekpe* and *Righetti* were re-read against current CanLII full text and checked against live official Court Files metadata on 2026-09-15 as respondent-success comparators.
3. This reference places *Agyemang* and *Odobo* alongside the later, materially different *Vasilev* analysis so the reference does not flatten a divided Federal Court line into a universal full-disclosure rule. This is later Federal Court reasoning, not appellate treatment, and does not suggest that one Federal Court decision hierarchically controls another. *Ekpe* is separately identified as a direct factual/doctrinal comparator to *Vasilev*.
4. *Chater* is placed alongside *Righetti* because both address omitted prior visa/permit refusals and materiality, but they reached different results on different reasons and records. Neither case is presented as hierarchically controlling the other.
5. Current IRPA/IRPR text and current IRCC procedural-fairness guidance were rechecked before publication. Departmental guidance is separated from legislation and from judicial holdings.
6. Live Federal Court Court Files controls were checked for the seven dockets, related-case records and bounded Federal Court of Appeal public-registry party searches through the research cutoff date.
7. Current later-treatment checks were completed before publication. Future readers should still verify current treatment before relying on any proposition in a live matter.
